Steel Town

How can a business advantage grow into market power?

Context and setup · 2 minutes

A growing country needs steel.

After the Civil War, expanding railroads connected more places to a national market. New industrial methods helped businesses produce goods on a larger scale. Steel helped build railroads, bridges and buildings.

Some owners built large companies, bought competitors or brought businesses under common control. Growth could support more production and lower costs. It also raised questions about competition and the influence of business in government.

What this simulation is trying to show

You will test how lower costs can produce more profit, how profit can fund expansion, and why control of an industry matters to customers and competitors.

Your goal: try to earn the most profit for your company. Then explain the advantages and tradeoffs you notice.

You are steel-factory owners.

Your teacher will assign your group one of six companies. Your goal is to earn as much profit as you can for that company over two rounds. Each round, discuss your price and tell your teacher: $4 or $5 per bundle. Decide only for your company. Your teacher enters all six choices on the projected screen, then reveals the results.

Understand your market

Your customers are businesses building railroads, bridges and buildings. Total market demand is 12 bundles of steel per round. All customers together want 12 bundles—not 12 from each company. A bundle is an invented unit of steel in this game.

All six firms sell the same kind of steel. Together, they can supply 20 bundles, but customers want only 12. That means some capacity will go unused. Customers buy from the lowest-price firms first; firms charging the same price split orders according to their capacity.

Why does Atlas start ahead?

Atlas already has a bigger mill and more efficient equipment. It can fill more orders and make each bundle for $2, while rivals spend $3. At a $4 selling price, Atlas earns $2 per sale; each smaller firm earns $1. These starting advantages are built into the game.

This is a model. The companies, dollar amounts and orders are invented. The model highlights one possible path toward market power; it does not reproduce an actual steel market or show that one outcome was inevitable.

How this market works
  1. Total market demand is 12 bundles. All customers combined will buy 12 bundles this round. Each bundle counts as one order.
  2. They buy from the lowest-price companies first, up to those companies’ capacity.
  3. If companies charge the same price, they divide orders according to how much each can produce. A bigger mill gets a bigger share. Whole-order rounding can create a difference of one order.
  4. Profit = (selling price − cost per bundle) × bundles sold. Unsold capacity earns nothing.

Prices and costs are dollars per bundle. Capacity is the maximum bundles a mill can supply in one round. The game counts only the costs shown; it leaves out taxes, debt, fixed costs and changes in demand.

Debrief · 3 minutes

From business advantage to market power

Lower cost → more profit → money to expand → an advantage in the next round.

When profit is enough to buy the upgrade, this loop becomes visible. Real outcomes also depend on technology, management, demand, workers, financing and competitors’ choices.

  1. Which advantage grew? Use one number from the results to explain how Atlas Steel’s starting advantage affected the second round.
  2. Who might benefit, and who might face pressure? Consider the buyers paying $4 or $5 and the businesses competing for orders. Selling more cheaply is not automatically unfair.
  3. What changes if the six mills have one owner? Compare the two ownership pictures below. Why would fewer independent sellers matter?

Same mills. Different control.

Bridge to The Bosses of the Senate

Business owners might seek government decisions that help their industry or the public, or decisions that favor their own companies. A business advantage does not itself give someone the authority to pass laws.

Now investigate: How does Keppler portray the relationship between business wealth and lawmakers? Look for his visual evidence before deciding what he means.

What this model leaves out

Atlas begins with a bigger mill and lower costs. Any company earning at least $8 can buy the fictional upgrade. Atlas has an advantage, but whether it can expand depends on the price choices and orders it wins. Large firms do not always win.

Equal-price orders are distributed by capacity, rather than by brand, location or personal preference. Buyers always need 12 bundles, so the model does not measure how demand responds to prices. It does not simulate workers’ wages, environmental costs, lobbying or corruption.

The “one owner” picture is a separate hypothetical ownership change, not an event that your prices caused. The picture changes who controls the mills; it does not calculate a future price.
